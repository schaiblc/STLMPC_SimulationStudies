Editor and Reviewer comments:



Reviewer #1: This manuscript presents a well-structured and practically relevant local planning framework for safe navigation in unknown, dynamic, and racing environments. The idea of generating successive tracking lines and integrating them with MPC is interesting, and the paper addresses an important problem with clear application value. The overall methodology is promising, and the experimental validation is fairly extensive. However, several parts of the manuscript would benefit from clearer explanation and more precise notation, particularly in the formulation of some equations, the definition of certain parameters, and the description of implementation details needed for reproducibility. In its current form, the technical contribution is meaningful, but the presentation still contains a number of ambiguities that may hinder readers' understanding. I therefore recommend **minor revision**, mainly to improve clarity, notation consistency, and reproducibility.

1. Equation (2) appears to use a normalized line form $w_i^T q + 1 = 0$, presumably obtained from $w^T q + b = 0$ by normalization with respect to (b). However, this transformation is only mentioned briefly and the exact definition of the normalized (w_i) is not given, which makes the appearance of the constant 1 in (2) unclear. Moreover, if the normalization indeed divides by (b), the manuscript should explain how the case $b=0$ is excluded or handled.
2. Equations (16a)-(16b) use the notation (\Delta v_{\max}), but the constraints are written as (\pm \Delta t,\Delta v_{\max}). This makes the meaning of (\Delta v_{\max}) ambiguous: if it denotes a maximum velocity increment, multiplying by (\Delta t) is inappropriate; if it denotes a maximum acceleration/deceleration, the notation should be changed accordingly (e.g., (a_{\max})). The current notation may confuse readers and should be clarified for dimensional consistency."

3. Equation (15) only enforces (v_i \ge 0), with (v_{\min}=0), while the added objective term in (14) is (F_{v^2}=\sum 1/v_i^2). This creates a potential singularity and numerical instability when (v_i) becomes very small, and the term is undefined at (v_i=0). The manuscript should clearly explain how this issue is avoided in practice. For example, does the implementation impose a strictly positive lower bound (v_{\min}>0), use a regularized form such as (1/(v_i^2+\varepsilon)), or apply some other safeguard? Without such clarification, it is unclear whether the method remains well-posed at low speeds, especially in start-from-rest or near-stop scenarios, where the vehicle may fail to leave a parking area or other confined environment.
4. The manuscript introduces (\theta_{\mathrm{band,max}}) and (s_\theta) in the obstacle-proximity velocity limiting formulation, but their numerical values are not provided in the paper. Since these parameters directly affect which obstacles are considered relevant in front of the vehicle and how sharply the bandpass weighting transitions, the authors should report their chosen values and explain how they were selected. Otherwise, the method is difficult to reproduce and the practical effect of these parameters is hard to assess.
5. Equation (19) is somewhat difficult to interpret in its current form, because it appears to combine the angular term and the obstacle distance into a single composite quantity without sufficient explanation. From the formulation, (d_{\mathrm{bandmin},i}) is not a geometric distance obtained by directly merging angle and range, but rather an angularly weighted soft minimum of obstacle distances, where (\theta_{\mathrm{band},i,j}) acts as a front-field relevance weight. The authors should clarify this interpretation explicitly in the manuscript so that the physical meaning of the expression is easier to understand. In addition, it would be very helpful to include either a contour plot or a 3D surface plot to visualize how the combined coefficient varies with obstacle angle and obstacle distance. Such a figure would make the influence of both variables much more intuitive and would significantly improve the readability of this part of the paper.
6. The manuscript states that the MPC problem is solved using an SQP-based SLSQP solver, and Algorithm 2 also refers to "SLSQP Opt." However, the implementation details of the solver are not sufficiently documented for reproducibility. The authors should clarify which specific SLSQP implementation or software package is used, together with the main solver settings, such as stopping tolerances, maximum iterations, and any time-limit or feasibility-related termination criteria. Since solver behavior can significantly affect convergence, numerical robustness, and real-time performance, providing these details would improve the transparency and reproducibility of the proposed method.



Reviewer #2: This paper develops an STLMPC standalone local MPC planner. Despite the complete system framework and physical RC vehicle experiments, the manuscript has severe deficiencies in novelty demonstration, theoretical rigor, experimental adequacy and reproducibility, as summarized below:
1. The paper only briefly cites gap-based path planners, SVM path generation methods and mainstream racing MPC algorithms without systematic horizontal quantitative comparison. It fails to elaborate the essential advantages of sequential multi-stage tracking lines over single reference line schemes, and cannot highlight the unique superiority of the proposed dual soft velocity constraints compared with existing racing speed adjustment strategies, making core contributions ambiguous and weakly supported.
2. The non-convex MPC problem solved via SLSQP lacks closed-loop receding horizon stability proof, solver convergence analysis and risk discussion on local optimal solutions. The manually designed feasible initial guess only relies on empirical experience with no theoretical justification. Multiple oversimplified modeling assumptions are adopted without error sensitivity analysis: dynamic obstacles are predicted under constant velocity and curvature hypothesis, while the performance degradation caused by rapid acceleration and sharp turning prediction deviation is not discussed; the pure kinematic bicycle model neglects tire slip and lateral vehicle dynamics, without evaluating model mismatch risks under high-speed aggressive racing maneuvers; the planner does not conduct anti-interference analysis for LiDAR sparse point clouds, sensor noise and partial obstacle occlusion.
3. The algorithm relies on dozens of manually tuned hyperparameters including loss weights, soft constraint steepness and safety thresholds, yet no parameter sensitivity ablation experiment is provided to prove the rationality of the parameter configuration and the superiority of smooth soft constraints against hard threshold limits. When modeling dynamic vehicles by discretizing body outlines into sampling points, the quantitative tradeoff between sampling density, computational overhead and collision safety margin is not analyzed. Standard unified risk metrics such as Time-to-Collision are missing to quantitatively measure the safety margin of dynamic obstacle avoidance behaviors.
4. The physical tests only adopt three manually constructed simple tracks, lacking validation on challenging complex scenes such as dense multi-vehicle interactive overtaking, narrow crowded passages, heavy target occlusion and low-light conditions. Simulation results are merely mentioned without detailed visualization and quantitative analysis, which cannot fully verify the generalization capability of STLMPC. The benchmark comparison only includes traditional ROS local planners and a simple PD controller, without introducing state-of-the-art gap-based MPC and lightweight MPPI racing planners for fair comparison. No modular ablation experiments are carried out to separately quantify the performance gain brought by sequential tracking lines, dynamic obstacle prediction branch and racing velocity constraints, so the independent contribution of each core component cannot be clarified.
